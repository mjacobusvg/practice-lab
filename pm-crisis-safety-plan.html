Crisis Safety Plan Builder

Collaborative safety planning using the Stanley-Brown model. Generates a patient handout and chart-ready documentation.

Clinical tool, not a substitute for judgment. This tool helps structure a safety plan during a clinical encounter. The clinician is responsible for all clinical decisions, risk assessment, and determining the appropriate level of care. All outputs should be reviewed before use. No patient data is stored after document generation.

Step 2 of 7
Step 1: Warning Signs
"What are the signs that a crisis is starting for you? What do you notice first?"
Clinical prompt: Ask about specific thoughts ("I can't take this anymore"), emotions (hopelessness, feeling trapped), physical sensations (chest tightness, restlessness), behaviors (isolating, increased substance use), and situations (being alone at night, after arguments). Document 3 to 5 specific warning signs in the patient's own words.
Step 3 of 7
Step 2: Internal Coping Strategies
"What can you do on your own, without contacting anyone, to get through the next few minutes?"
Clinical prompt: These are things the patient can do alone to manage the moment. Examples: listen to a specific playlist, take a shower, go for a walk, play with a pet, deep breathing, watch a specific show, journal. Specificity matters -- "listen to music" is less useful than "listen to my calm playlist on Spotify."
Step 4 of 7
Step 3: Social Settings and People for Distraction
"Where can you go or who can you be around, without necessarily talking about how you're feeling?"
Clinical prompt: The goal is distraction, not disclosure. Examples: coffee shop, friend's house ("hang out with Jamie"), gym, library, dog park. These are places and people that shift the patient out of isolation without requiring them to talk about suicidal thoughts.
Step 5 of 7
Step 4: People to Contact for Help
"Who can you call when you need to talk about how you're feeling?"
Clinical prompt: These are people the patient can disclose to -- trusted friends, family members, sponsors, mentors. Include name and phone number. Consider asking: "Who have you talked to before when things got hard? Who in your life would want to know if you were struggling?"
Step 6 of 7
Step 5: Professionals and Agencies
Professional contacts and crisis resources the patient can reach.
These are standard crisis resources. Edit or add local resources as needed.
Step 7 of 7
Step 6: Making the Environment Safe
Lethal means restriction. Document what actions are being taken to reduce access to means.
Clinical prompt: Lethal means counseling is one of the most evidence-supported suicide prevention interventions. Ask directly about firearms, medications, and other means. Document the specific plan for each. Involving a trusted person in means restriction increases effectiveness.
Firearms
Unsecured firearms are the highest-risk means factor. Document the specific plan and timeline for securing or removing firearms. Consider whether the current level of care is appropriate.
Medications
Document the specific medications of concern and the plan to reduce access. Consider shorter prescription fills, involving a family member in medication management, or lock boxes.
Other Means
Ligatures, sharp objects, access to heights, or other means identified during risk assessment.
