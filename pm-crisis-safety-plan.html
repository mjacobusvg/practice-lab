Step 2 of 9
Suicide Risk Assessment
Structured inquiry results. These drive your chart documentation and risk formulation.

Opening the conversation

"I need to ask you some direct questions about thoughts of suicide. Have you been thinking about killing yourself?"

Do not soften with euphemisms. Do not ask leading questions that invite denial (e.g., "You're not thinking about hurting yourself, are you?"). Be clear and direct. Research shows asking about suicide does not increase risk -- patients frequently report feeling validated when asked directly.

When to assess even if screening is negative

If risk factors are present (recent loss, substance use, prior attempt, behavioral warning signs, recent hospitalization, high-risk clinical context like intoxication or psychosis), conduct a full assessment even if the screen is negative. Trust your clinical judgment. A negative screen does not guarantee safety.

Passive = wish to be dead without thoughts of killing oneself. Active = thoughts of killing oneself.

Questions to ask

"How often are you having these thoughts? How long do they last? On a scale of 0 to 10, how intense are they right now?"

You are assessing whether ideation is chronic vs. acute, escalating or stable, and how much control the patient feels they have.

Red flags

Increasing frequency. Thoughts lasting longer. Intensity 7+. "I can't get the thoughts out of my head." New onset in a previously stable patient.

Ambivalence is protective. If a patient expresses zero ambivalence, risk escalates significantly.

Intent questions

"Do you intend to act on these thoughts? Are you planning to kill yourself? If you left here today and went home, would you carry out this plan?"

Preparatory behavior questions

"Have you done anything to prepare? Have you written a note, given away belongings, said goodbye to anyone, researched methods, or obtained means?"

Preparatory behaviors are the strongest indicator of imminent risk. A patient who has taken steps toward suicide is at acute risk and requires immediate intervention.

Intoxication + SI = high-risk combination. Stimulant crash and alcohol disinhibition both escalate risk.
Step 3 of 9
Protective Factors
Factors that reduce the likelihood of suicidal behavior. These provide leverage points for intervention.

Questions to ask

"What keeps you safe when you feel this way?"

"What reasons do you have for living right now?"

"What would stop you from going through with it?"

What to look for

Internal: reasons for living (children, pets, goals, spirituality), coping skills, hope, fear of death/suicide, self-efficacy.

External: social connection, therapeutic alliance, access to care, restricted access to means, cultural/religious beliefs, meaningful activities.

Red flags

Cannot identify any reasons for living. Dismisses previously important protective factors ("My kids would be better off without me"). States protective factors are weakening. These indicate cognitive reframing of protective factors and escalate risk.

This is your clinical judgment. Risk stratification categories guide intervention intensity, not prediction of outcomes.
This rationale appears verbatim in the chart documentation. Be specific and defensible.

If proceeding with safety planning, document your reasoning for outpatient management.
Step 4 of 9
Step 1: Warning Signs
"What are the signs that a crisis is starting for you? What do you notice first?"

How to prompt the patient

"What are the signs that a suicidal crisis is starting for you? What do you notice first?"

Categories to explore

Thoughts: "I can't take this anymore," "Everyone would be better off without me," "There's no point."

Emotions: Feeling numb, hopeless, trapped, enraged, overwhelmed.

Physical sensations: Chest tightness, racing heart, exhaustion, restlessness.

Behaviors: Isolating, not answering texts, drinking more, not sleeping, staring at pills or firearms.

Situations: Being alone at night, after arguments, financial stress, anniversary dates.

What if the patient says "I don't know"?

This is common. Walk them through it: "Think about the last time you felt really bad. What did you notice first? Was it a thought? A feeling in your body? Something that happened?" Help them identify the earliest signal. Specificity matters -- "feeling bad" is too vague. "Thinking about my gun when I'm alone at night" is actionable.

Step 5 of 9
Step 2: Internal Coping Strategies
"What can you do on your own, without contacting anyone, to get through the next few minutes?"

How to prompt the patient

"What are things you can do on your own to get through the next few minutes or hours when you're feeling suicidal? What has worked before, even a little?"

Examples

Listen to a specific playlist. Watch a specific show ("Watch The Office, Season 3" not "watch TV"). Take a shower. Go for a walk. Pet or play with an animal. Do a puzzle, draw, or journal. Deep breathing or 5-4-3-2-1 grounding exercise. Cook or bake something. Clean or organize a space. Read.

Key principle

Specificity matters. "Distract myself" is too vague. "Listen to my Calm playlist on Spotify" is specific and actionable. Help the patient identify what has actually worked before, even partially.

Step 6 of 9
Step 3: Social Settings and People for Distraction
"Where can you go or who can you be around, without necessarily talking about how you're feeling?"

The goal here is distraction, not disclosure. The patient is not telling these people about suicidal thoughts. They are simply breaking isolation and being around others.

Examples

Go to a coffee shop and sit with a laptop. Visit a friend or family member. Go to the gym or a fitness class. Attend a church service or community group. Go to a park or public space. Call a friend and talk about something neutral (sports, TV, etc.).

What if the patient is isolated?

Public spaces count. A coffee shop, library, or park puts the patient around other people even without social interaction. The goal is to move the patient out of the physical environment where suicidal thoughts intensify.

Step 7 of 9
Step 4: People to Contact for Help
"Who can you call when you need to talk about how you're feeling?"

These are people the patient can disclose to -- trusted friends, family members, sponsors, mentors, spiritual advisors. Include name and phone number so the patient does not have to search during a crisis.

Prompts

"Who have you talked to before when things got hard? Who in your life would want to know if you were struggling?"

What if the patient can't identify anyone?

This is a significant finding and should be documented as a risk-amplifying factor (social isolation). Consider whether the patient needs a higher level of care or whether crisis resources alone are sufficient for Step 5.

Step 8 of 9
Step 5: Professionals and Agencies
Professional contacts and crisis resources the patient can reach.

Many solo practitioners do not have a formal after-hours line. Your options:

1. Answering service: Contracted services that triage calls and page you for emergencies. Costs approximately $50-150/month. This is the most common solution for solo practices.

2. Covering provider agreement: Arrange with a colleague to cover after-hours calls. Document the arrangement formally.

3. 988 as primary after-hours resource: You can instruct patients to call 988 (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) after hours. Make sure the patient understands when to call you vs. 988 vs. go to the ED.

4. Voicemail with crisis instructions: At minimum, your voicemail should include: "If this is a mental health emergency, please call 988, go to your nearest emergency department, or call 911."

If you leave this field blank, the patient handout will include instructions to call 988 or go to the nearest ED for after-hours crises. The chart documentation will note that after-hours coverage was addressed via crisis resources.

Step 9 of 9
Step 6: Making the Environment Safe
Lethal means restriction. This is the most important step of the safety plan.

Why this matters

Means restriction is one of the most evidence-supported suicide prevention interventions. Suicidal crises are often brief -- nearly half of patients in one study reported 10 minutes or less between onset of suicidal thinking and the attempt. When means are not immediately available, that window can allow the crisis to pass.

Method substitution is not inevitable. Restricting access to highly lethal methods has not typically resulted in proportional increases in suicide by other methods. People delay or abandon attempts rather than switching.

The 10-minute conversation framework

Minutes 1-2: "One of the most important things we can do to keep you safe is to reduce access to things you could use to harm yourself. Research shows this saves lives. Can we talk about what you have at home?"

Minutes 3-5: "Do you have firearms? Medications? Ropes or cords? What else?"

Minutes 6-8: "Let's figure out how to remove or secure these things. Who can help? Can we call them now?"

Minutes 9-10: "So to make sure I understand: [summarize plan]. When will this happen? Can you commit to this?"

Key principles

Be direct and matter-of-fact. Frame as temporary and collaborative, not punitive. Involve others when possible. Set a clear timeline (ideally same-day action). Follow up at next visit to confirm implementation.

🔫 Firearms

Always ask

"Do you have firearms at home? Where are they stored? Are they locked? Are they loaded? Who else has access?"

Firearms are the most lethal method of suicide (85-90% fatality rate). In Washington, firearms are used in approximately 50% of suicide deaths. There is no time for reconsideration or rescue.

Language to use

"Right now, while you're feeling this way, it's really important that firearms are not easily accessible. This isn't forever. It's just to get you through this crisis safely. Can we talk about how to make that happen?"

Options for restriction

Off-site storage: Family member, friend, or gun range holds firearms temporarily.

Locked storage: Firearms locked in a safe; someone else holds the key or combination.

Disassembly: Removing firing pins or ammunition and storing separately (less ideal).

Law enforcement hold: Some jurisdictions allow voluntary temporary surrender to police.

If the patient refuses

Explore their concerns: "What concerns do you have about removing the firearms temporarily? What would make this easier? Would you be willing to at least lock them up and give the key to someone else?"

If patient has active intent + specific plan involving firearm + refuses removal, this typically requires higher-level care. Moderate risk + firearm refusal may be acceptable outpatient IF patient has protective factors, engagement, and intensive follow-up. High risk + firearm refusal = not safe for outpatient management.

Washington ERPO information

Washington allows family members, household members, and law enforcement to petition a court for an Extreme Risk Protection Order (ERPO) to temporarily remove firearms from individuals at risk (RCW 7.105). Clinicians cannot petition but can educate families that the option exists.

Important: Clinicians should never physically take or store patient firearms. All arrangements must be coordinated by the patient or family.

Unsecured firearms with identified suicidal ideation is the highest-risk means factor. Document the specific plan and timeline for securing or removing firearms. Consider whether the current level of care is appropriate.
💊 Medications

"Do you have medications at home that you could take to harm yourself? Are you stockpiling pills?"

High-risk medications to specifically ask about

Opioids, benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants (highly lethal in overdose), lithium, acetaminophen and NSAIDs in large quantities.

Options

Reduce quantities: Limit prescription fills to 7-14 day supplies during high-risk periods. Document your rationale.

Involve family: Ask spouse/roommate to dispense medications daily.

Dispose of excess: Take unused medications to a drug take-back program.

Lock medications: Use a lockbox; someone else holds the key.

Document the specific medications of concern and the plan to reduce access. Consider shorter prescription fills, involving a family member in medication management, or lock boxes.
⚠️ Other Means

Ligatures (hanging): Second most common method. Highly lethal (70-85%). Requires minimal planning.

"Have you thought about hanging? Do you have ropes, belts, or cords at home?"

Counseling: Remove or secure ropes, extension cords, belts. Involve family in removing ligature materials. Avoid being alone in spaces where hanging could occur (basements, garages, closets).

Vehicles: If plan involves carbon monoxide or intentional crash, involve family in monitoring vehicle access.

Heights: If plan involves jumping, avoid access to bridges, buildings, or other high places.

Water: If plan involves drowning, avoid being alone near bodies of water.

Document what follow-up the patient agreed to. This appears in the chart documentation.
