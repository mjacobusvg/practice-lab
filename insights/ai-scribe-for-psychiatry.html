AI Scribe for Psychiatry — Why Ours Reasons Instead of Just Transcribing

By Michael Van Gelder, M.S., M.N., PMHNP-BC · Board-certified Psychiatric Mental Health Nurse Practitioner

The AI scribe market is crowded, and almost every tool does the same thing: it listens to the visit and turns the conversation into text. For a psychiatric prescriber, that is not a note. A defensible psychiatric note is a record of what you decided and why — the history, the mental status, the risk assessment, the medication rationale, the intervention, and the billing all of it supports. The gap between "what was said in the room" and "what you decided" is exactly where thin documentation, audit risk, and lost revenue live. That gap is the difference between an ambient dictation tool and what we built.

What Most AI Scribes Actually Do

Most AI medical scribes are ambient transcription tools. They listen, transcribe, and format the result into a template. That is genuinely useful for turning a rambling visit into a readable paragraph — but it captures what was spoken, not what was clinically determined. In psychiatry, those are rarely the same thing.

Two problems follow from that design:

They are built for general medicine

Psychiatric documentation has its own demands — a mental status exam, risk assessment, medication rationale, psychotherapy add-on elements, and medical decision-making that has to hold up to review. In a general-purpose scribe, those are an afterthought bolted onto a primary-care template.

They fill in what they didn't hear

To make a note read smoothly, many scribes generate plausible-sounding content that was never actually established — a tidy "patient denies suicidal ideation" when the question was never asked, or a therapy technique described that you never used. In most specialties that is a convenience. In psychiatry, an invented negative or a documented intervention that did not happen is a liability, not a time-saver.

What Ours Does Differently: It Reasons, and It Refuses to Invent

Our AI Scribe is built for psychiatric prescribers, and it is built around a different premise: the note should reflect your clinical reasoning, and it should contain only what you actually established.

It structures your reasoning, not just your words

Instead of formatting a transcript, it organizes the visit the way a prescriber thinks and the way an auditor reads — a coherent HPI, a mental status exam, an assessment that names the target, and a plan with the rationale behind each decision.

It will not put words in your note

The governing rule is simple: if you did not establish it, it does not appear. No invented findings, no assumed negatives, no "considered" techniques you never used, no answers to questions you never asked. What you charted becomes the note; the scaffolding that helped you get there never masquerades as evidence. For a document that is both medico-legal and billable, that restraint is the entire point.

It writes in your template, not a generic house style

It works from your own structure and language — your saved template — rather than forcing every note into one vendor's format. The output reads like your documentation, because it is.

Documentation That Is Already Defensible — and Already Coded

Because the scribe lives alongside a chart auditor and coder, the note is not just readable — it is checked against what the code actually requires. If you provided psychotherapy, the note shows the specific elements a 90833 needs. If your medical decision-making supports a 99214, the note shows why. The most common way good clinicians lose money and take on audit risk is a note that reads well but does not support the code. A scribe that understands the coding closes that gap before it becomes a denial.

Dictation vs. Reasoning: A Concrete Difference

Say the visit amounts to: "We talked through her anxiety at work, I reinforced some cognitive strategies, and I increased the sertraline."

A dictation scribe turns that into a smooth paragraph that reads fine and supports almost nothing. A reasoning scribe produces a structured HPI, an assessment that names the psychological target, the rationale for the dose change, the psychotherapy element documented to the standard 90833 requires if you actually did that work — and it flags the code the note supports. Same visit, two very different documents. One is a summary. The other is defensible.

Built Under a BAA, Inside a Whole Practice System

PHI is handled under a Business Associate Agreement — not pasted into a consumer chatbot. And the scribe is not a standalone bolt-on: it sits inside the same platform as your billing tools, your clinical references, and a community of prescribers solving the same documentation problems. The note you generate connects to the rest of how you run your practice.

An AI scribe that reasons — and refuses to invent — protects the two things a transcript can't: your license and your revenue. That is the standard we built to.
