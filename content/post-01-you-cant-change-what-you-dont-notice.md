When I was much younger, I thought mindfulness was supposed to be something almost metaphysical.

It sat somewhere on the fringe between psychology, religion, spirituality and meditation. I would try to meditate, try to clear my mind, and mostly end up frustrated because nothing happened.

I kept waiting for whatever it was I was apparently supposed to experience. Some special state. Some profound calm. Maybe enlightenment was running late.

Then I accumulated a great deal of psychology. Psychology in high school. An undergraduate degree in it. A master's in clinical psychology. More psychology and psychotherapy coursework in PMHNP school. Classes on therapeutic modalities, theories, historical figures, behavior, cognition, emotion, development.

Mindfulness kept appearing in all of it.

For a long time I am not sure I really understood why it mattered.

What eventually made it click came back through the same territory I had always associated it with. Reading The Power of Now. Reading Thich Nhat Hanh. Then coming back around to mindfulness through psychotherapy, awareness, behavior and change.

And somewhere in there I stopped thinking of mindfulness as a state you are supposed to achieve, and started thinking about it as something much more ordinary. Noticing what is happening while it is happening.

Even that is not quite right.

# Awareness is not the same as insight

We use awareness, insight and mindfulness almost interchangeably. I do not think they are the same thing.

Someone can have awareness with very little insight.

I feel anxious.

My chest is tight.

My thoughts are racing.

I keep checking.

That is real, and it is limited. Noticing something does not tell you what it means, what it is doing for you, or why it keeps happening.

Insight adds the understanding.

I check when I feel uncertain.

Reassurance settles it for a few minutes.

I rehearse conversations because I am trying not to be embarrassed.

I look up symptoms because not knowing what a sensation means feels intolerable.

Now the person understands something about the pattern.

And insight has its own limit. We have all met the patient who can explain their pattern beautifully. They can name the trigger. They can describe the reinforcement. They can predict exactly what they are about to do.

And then they do it anyway.

Knowing about a pattern afterward is a different thing from recognizing it while you are inside it.

The way I have come to think about it clinically is:

**Awareness without insight is observation.**

**Insight without awareness is hindsight.**

**Mindfulness is when awareness and insight meet in the present moment.**

# Awareness within awareness

The phrase I find myself using is awareness within awareness. Meta-awareness is the more technical term, but it does not convey the experience as well.

You are not only having the thought. You recognize that you are having the thought.

You are not only feeling the urge. You notice: there is the urge.

It is a little like Inception, except instead of dreams within dreams, it is awareness within awareness.

Put the three next to each other and the difference is easier to see.

Awareness: I am anxious.

Insight: When I am anxious and uncertain, I ask for reassurance, because it makes the uncertainty go away for a while.

And the one we are actually after: I am anxious, and right now I can feel myself wanting to ask for reassurance. I know what this is.

Read that last one again and notice the tense.

**The behavior has not happened yet.**

That is the whole difference. Not an easy moment, and not some magical freedom from the anxiety. The person may desperately want the reassurance. But for a moment they are two things at once. The person having the urge, and the person watching themselves have it.

And in that moment there is a choice point that did not exist a second earlier.

# If you have ever lucid dreamed

If you have ever had a lucid dream, it is a similar experience.

You are still dreaming. The dream does not stop. Whatever is happening in it may still feel completely real. And then there is this moment:

Wait. I am dreaming.

You have not woken up. You are still inside it. But your relationship to it changes.

And you realize you have a little more control than you did a moment before, when you were simply being carried along by the dream. Not total control. More influence. More say in what happens next instead of just going along with the motions.

That is very close to what this feels like in real life.

You are still anxious. You still have the thought. You still feel the urge to check, avoid, reassure, escape, argue or react.

But now another layer comes online.

Wait. I am anxious right now.

Or: there is the urge to check.

Or: I am starting to do the thing I always do when I feel uncertain.

You are having the experience and noticing yourself have it, at the same time.

# And we do not stay there

Here is the part that matters, and the part that would have saved me years of frustration on a meditation cushion.

Just like lucid dreaming, we do not stay lucid.

You can become lucid in a dream and then get pulled straight back into it and forget you ever knew. The same thing happens awake. We notice ourselves worrying, or rehearsing, or checking, and five minutes later we are completely absorbed in it again.

Then: oh. I got pulled back in.

And in that moment, you are back.

So mindfulness is not a stable elevated state you finally reach and then live in. It is not a monk sitting in perfect uninterrupted awareness. We move in and out of it all day.

Which gives you the actual practice, and it is almost a paradox:

**The goal is not to never lose awareness. The practice is noticing that you lost it.**

Because the moment you recognize that you have been completely caught up in something for the last ten minutes, you are already back. And with practice, ten minutes becomes five. Five becomes thirty seconds. Eventually, sometimes, you catch the urge as it is forming.

Mindfulness is awareness of awareness in the present moment. Not because you have escaped the experience, but because for a moment you are not completely fused with it.

# Control is not quite the word, but it is close

Control gets tricky in anxiety treatment.

If mindfulness becomes another technique for controlling thoughts, suppressing feelings or making anxiety go away, we have quietly turned it into another safety behavior. That is not what this is.

It does not give us control over which thoughts appear. It does not give us control over fear. It does not make uncomfortable sensations stop.

What it can give us is more say in what happens next.

I may not be able to choose whether an intrusive thought shows up. I may not be able to choose whether my heart races. I may not be able to choose whether uncertainty feels awful.

But if I recognize what is happening while it is happening, I have more choice about whether I check, escape, reassure myself, ask someone else, start researching, rehearse, or run the ritual that usually follows.

Agency is probably the better word. Mindfulness does not give us control over the experience. It gives us some agency inside it.

That is where change becomes possible.

# The catch is that this gets harder exactly when we need it

The moment this kind of awareness would be most useful is often the moment it is hardest to reach.

When the brain registers threat, the defensive systems are built to act fast. Attention narrows. The body mobilizes. Fight, flight, freeze, escape, scanning and fast habitual responses take over.

There is a good reason for that. If something dangerous is coming at you, pausing to thoughtfully examine your internal experience is not the adaptive move. You want the organism to react.

The problem in anxiety is not that we have a threat system. It is that the same system fires for situations, sensations, thoughts, memories and uncertainty that do not actually call for what it is demanding.

People call this an amygdala hijack. That is useful shorthand, though the brain is more complicated than one structure seizing control, and the old lizard brain framing is too simple to be much help.

The clinically useful version is simpler than either.

When threat activation is intense, reflective awareness gets harder.

Someone in the middle of a panic attack is not thinking, I notice significant autonomic activation and an urge to engage in a safety behavior. They are thinking:

Something is wrong.

Get out.

Check.

Make sure.

Do something.

Which is why telling someone to just be mindful in the middle of overwhelming fear is close to useless if they have never practiced noticing themselves anywhere else.

# You practice it before you need it

This is the actual reason mindfulness gets practiced when nothing much is happening.

Not because noticing your breath while sitting still is the point. It is rehearsal.

You learn what it feels like to notice a thought as a thought. A sensation as a sensation. An urge as an urge. You learn the difference between having an experience and immediately responding to it.

And then, hopefully, it starts to generalize, and it shows up earlier in the sequence.

At first you see it afterward: I spent two hours researching because I was anxious.

Then: I have been researching for twenty minutes because I am anxious.

Then: I have been doing this for five minutes.

And eventually, sometimes: there it is. I just felt the urge to start researching.

That difference is enormous. The earlier the awareness lands in the sequence, the more room there is for a different response.

It is the difference between waking up after the dream and becoming lucid while you are still in it.

# You cannot change what you do not notice

This is why I think awareness sits underneath so many different therapies.

CBT asks people to notice thoughts, interpretations and behavior.

ERP asks them to notice the trigger, the distress, the urge and the ritual without automatically neutralizing it.

ACT asks them to recognize thoughts and feelings without being completely fused with them.

DBT teaches mindfulness outright, because noticing what is happening internally is what makes choosing a skill possible instead of reacting.

Motivational interviewing depends on people hearing their own ambivalence.

Different theories. Different language. Different interventions. But something has to become visible before anyone can deliberately do something else with it.

That does not mean awareness produces change on its own. It does not. Insight does not either. Someone can understand themselves extremely well and stay completely stuck, and someone can be exquisitely aware of their suffering and have no idea what is keeping it going.

But when the two start operating together, in real time, something shifts. The patient is not only living inside the pattern. They are starting to see it while it is happening.

And now there is somewhere for the two of us to intervene.

That is the foundation for the rest of this series. Because once we get better at noticing what happens when anxiety shows up, the next question becomes the useful one:

What are we actually looking for?

In anxiety, one of the most consistent answers is avoidance. And avoidance does not always look like avoidance.

Sometimes it looks like checking. Sometimes reassurance. Sometimes preparation. Sometimes worry. Sometimes research. Sometimes a compulsion. Sometimes it looks like being responsible, or thorough, or careful.

So next we give this awareness a target.

Find the avoidance, find a treatment target.
