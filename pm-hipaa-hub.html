Step 2 of 8

Systems & Vendors

What technology does your practice use? This determines your BAA requirements and security documentation.

How do you communicate with or about patients where PHI might be transmitted? Include email, messaging platforms, portals, and any other channel.
Any channel used to transmit PHI needs a BAA. Free personal email (Gmail, Outlook, Yahoo) does not offer BAAs and is not HIPAA-compliant for transmitting PHI. Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 can be HIPAA-compliant if you accept the BAA in your admin settings. Platforms like Spruce and Doximity include BAAs by default. If you use free email only for scheduling with no clinical details, it is lower risk but should still be documented. (45 CFR 164.312(e) - Transmission Security, 45 CFR 164.502(e) - Business Associate Contracts)
Consumer versions of AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude.ai, Google Gemini, Otter.ai) do not offer BAAs and are not HIPAA-compliant for processing PHI. Pasting patient notes into consumer AI tools is a HIPAA violation. Enterprise or API versions of some platforms may offer BAAs, but the standard consumer product does not. If you are currently using a consumer AI tool for clinical documentation, stop immediately. Do not go back and delete notes already created (that creates audit issues). Document the date you stopped and switch to a HIPAA-compliant alternative like Suki, Nuance DAX, Abridge, or Freed, all of which offer BAAs specifically designed for clinical documentation. (45 CFR 164.502(e) - Business Associate Contracts)
Add any other services not listed above that touch patient information: answering services, transcription, e-prescribing (if separate from EHR), payment processors, etc.
Step 3 of 8

Devices & Security

Every device you use to access patient information needs to be documented with its security controls.

Include any device you use to log into your EHR, check patient messages, or access practice email.
If a device that accesses PHI is lost or stolen, the first question is whether it was encrypted. An encrypted, password-protected device that is stolen is generally not a reportable breach because the data cannot be accessed. An unencrypted device is almost always a reportable breach, even if the thief never looks at the data. Documenting your devices and their security controls is your defense. (45 CFR 164.310(d) - Device and Media Controls; 45 CFR 164.312(a)(2)(iv) - Encryption)

Telehealth Practices

If you provide telehealth, document your verification and security practices. If not, skip this step.

You indicated you do not provide telehealth. This section will be marked as "Not applicable" in your documentation. You can skip to the next step.

Environmental privacy is one of the most commonly overlooked telehealth compliance areas. Smart speakers passively listen for wake words and can inadvertently capture session audio. Household members walking past an open door or seeing a screen create incidental disclosure risks. These are not theoretical concerns. Documenting your environmental controls demonstrates that you have considered and mitigated these risks. (45 CFR 164.312(e) - Transmission Security; 45 CFR 164.530(c) - Safeguards)
Step 6 of 8

Workforce

Document anyone besides you who accesses patient information.

You indicated you are a solo provider with no staff. Your documentation will note: "No workforce members beyond the solo provider. Workforce training documentation not applicable." You can skip to the next step.

For each person who accesses PHI, enter their role and what they can access. This generates their access control documentation and determines whether they need a confidentiality agreement.
HIPAA requires that workforce members only access the minimum PHI necessary for their role. A billing contractor needs diagnosis codes and CPT codes but not psychotherapy notes. A family member who helps with scheduling needs patient names and appointment times but not clinical records. Documenting who can access what protects both you and your patients. It also protects you if a workforce member violates policy, because you can show you had controls in place. (45 CFR 164.502(b) - Minimum Necessary Standard; 45 CFR 164.308(a)(4) - Information Access Management)
Step 7 of 8

BAA Inventory

For each vendor you identified, confirm whether you have a signed Business Associate Agreement on file.

What is a BAA?

A Business Associate Agreement is a contract between you and any vendor that handles patient data on your behalf. It legally requires them to protect PHI. Without one, if the vendor has a data breach, you are responsible.

The good news: most vendors you already use (EHR, telehealth, email platforms) include a BAA automatically. You probably already "signed" it when you accepted their terms of service during setup. You just need to know where to find it.

If you are not sure: select "No" for any vendor you cannot confirm. The tool will show you exactly where to find or request the BAA for that vendor. You can always come back and update this later.

Without a BAA, a vendor has no legal obligation to protect your patients' data. If they have a breach and you do not have a BAA on file, you are responsible for the breach notification and any resulting penalties. Most major vendors provide BAAs automatically -- the key is knowing where they are and being able to produce them if asked during an audit or after an incident. If a vendor that handles PHI will not sign a BAA, do not use that vendor. (45 CFR 164.502(e) - Business Associate Contracts; 45 CFR 164.504(e) - BAA Requirements)
